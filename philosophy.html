A letter 06 / 09 / 2026

To those who find their way here,

We thought we might write a little about why this place exists, although we are not entirely sure that places such as this are best explained. Perhaps they are better found, the way you sometimes find a good address: somebody tells you about it after a conversation, writes it down on a piece of paper, and says that you should go.

You do not necessarily know what is there. You only know that someone whose judgement you trust thought it was worth the journey. Perhaps they had been talking about a road they travelled, a person they met, a coat that had been with them for twenty years, a strange little place to eat, a tree growing somewhere it should not, or a material they had found quite by accident.

We like the idea of an address being passed from one person to another in this way. Not advertised too loudly. Not immediately understood. Something you arrive at because you were curious enough to follow a thread.

What you find when you arrive is difficult to put into one category. There are clothes and accessories, of course, but also materials, patterns, samples, tools, unfinished ideas, things being tested, things being reconsidered, and things that may never become anything at all. There is work, but there is also conversation. There is making, but there is also looking, touching, thinking and sometimes deciding that the best thing to do is to wait.

We prefer beginning with a person rather than with a product.

When someone comes to see us, we want to know something about how they live. What they carry. Where they go. What they use every day. What they have kept for a long time. What they have worn until it became completely familiar. What they notice. What they do not care about. Sometimes even what they thought they were looking for turns out not to be what they need.

So we talk first.

There is no particular expectation that you should leave carrying something. You might look at a cloth, turn an object over in your hands, try something on, ask a question, or simply spend some time there. You might leave without buying anything at all. We are quite comfortable with that.

Sometimes there is another conversation. Sometimes an idea becomes clearer after a little distance. Perhaps the cloth is right but the colour is not. Perhaps a proportion needs changing. Perhaps something does not exist yet and has to be developed. Perhaps somebody elsewhere has already solved the problem exceptionally well, in which case we would rather find what they have made than reproduce it for the sake of saying that we made it ourselves.

We are interested in the product itself, perhaps more than is fashionable to admit. In construction. In proportion. In the behaviour of a material. In how something folds, moves, carries weight, breathes, ages and changes. In what happens after one year, and then after five, and then after ten.

The point is not complexity for its own sake, nor novelty for the pleasure of calling something new. Sometimes the simplest solution is the most difficult one to arrive at. We are interested in getting closer to the right thing, even when doing so takes longer than expected.

A few months can be a perfectly reasonable amount of time for something to become right. An idea may need to sit untouched. A material may need another attempt. A decision may be better made after everyone has gone home. We do not think time is an inconvenience that has to be hidden from the person waiting for the result. Time is part of the work.

And eventually, if something is made, it leaves.

It goes into somebody's life, which is where we think it should go. It is worn on an ordinary morning. It goes into a suitcase. It gets caught on something. It gets wet. It gets stained. It is forgotten for a while and then found again. It becomes associated with a journey, a particular year, a particular person.

This matters to us because we do not believe that a good object should become precious by remaining untouched. Quite the opposite. Some things become precious precisely because they have been lived with.

We are not interested in manufactured signs of age, or in making something look as though it has travelled when it has not. We like the marks that arrive honestly. The softened edge, the change in colour, the small imperfection caused by use. The quiet evidence that something has accompanied a person rather than simply survived in a cupboard.

And if something happens to it, come back. If it needs altering, come back. If it needs repairing, come back. If a piece has been with you long enough that it no longer fits the way it once did, that is not necessarily a reason to replace it. Perhaps it is simply time to change it again.

We would rather repair a good thing than replace it because replacement happens to be easier. We would rather maintain a relationship with an object than treat the moment of purchase as the end of one.

There is another part of this that is difficult to separate from the work itself.

We are very interested in the world that exists around objects. In images, but especially in images that do not seem desperate to be beautiful. In skin that looks like skin. In soft skin. In relief. In light that seems to have simply arrived rather than having been manufactured for the picture.

We like good proportions. We like fields and trees, endemic plants and old houses, roads, weather, dust, stone, the particular character of a place. We like things that already have their own existence and do not need to be made interesting by being placed inside a campaign.

Somewhere in our heads there is a place that perhaps does not yet exist, or perhaps never will in exactly this form. A workshop, maybe an office, on a large piece of land, surrounded by plants that belong to that particular place, perhaps inside an old house with another history.

We like the thought of putting things together that do not obviously belong together. Something very simple beside something extremely considered. Something old beside something newly developed. A good object beside a tree. Work beside stillness. The practical beside the beautiful, without having to decide which one is more important.

We like strange combinations when they make their own sense.

Perhaps this is also why we imagine certain people finding their way here.

People who have travelled without needing much. People who have taken a backpack, a car and a road and simply gone. People who have slept somewhere unfamiliar, learned something useful from somebody they had only just met, or spent an afternoon somewhere with nothing particular to accomplish.

People who might come from one place and belong to several others. People who speak several languages, or have more than one idea of home. People who understand why a tree matters without needing the explanation.

People who recognise a good object before somebody explains why it is good. Who want to turn it around, touch it, understand how it was made, see how it works and imagine what it will become after years of use.

Curious, but not restless. Adventurous, but comfortable with very little. Interested in the world without needing to decorate themselves with it. Natural in daily life. Comfortable with silence, with waiting, with things that do not immediately produce an answer.

Plastic bodies are not our DNA.

We mean that quite literally, but also as a way of describing an attitude. We are interested in things that have some relationship with the real world: material, body, weather, place, use, time. Things that can be touched. Things that can change. Things that carry evidence of having existed somewhere.

Perhaps that is why we have never imagined this as simply a shop. We imagine it more as a small oasis in a rather bizarre world. Somewhere you can arrive and, for a little while, allow time to become suspended.

You come in. You talk. You look at something. You touch it. Perhaps you learn something. Perhaps you change your mind. Perhaps you leave with nothing.

And perhaps, months later, something that began as a conversation, a material, a question or a strange idea finally becomes part of your life.

That is probably enough of an explanation. The rest is better understood by coming, looking around and seeing whether the place makes sense to you.

We do not think this is for everyone. It does not need to be.

But perhaps the people for whom it is will recognise something familiar in it.

And if you are one of them, perhaps somebody will give you the address.

Until then,
